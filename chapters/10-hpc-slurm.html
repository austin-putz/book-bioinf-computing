10  HPC and SLURM

Core. Work through it, with the exercises.

NoteLearning goals

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Tell a login node from a compute node, list a cluster’s partitions and your accounts, and say which work belongs where.
  2. Choose the right storage area for a file, and check your use against your quota (bytes, and files where the site counts them).
  3. Load software with module load or the book environment, and keep the two from clashing.
  4. Write and submit a batch job with sensible resource requests, then watch, read, and cancel it.
  5. Find out why a job is waiting or failed, measure what a finished job used, size the next request from it, and run one script over many inputs with a job array.

10.1 What this chapter does

So far you have used a compute node in one way only: by asking for a shell on it, with the srun line from Section 1.3. Real analyses run differently. You write a script, hand it to the cluster’s scheduler with a list of what it needs, and log out. The scheduler starts it when a node has room, runs it without you, and keeps a record of what happened. That script plus its request is a job, and the scheduler on Ceres, Atlas, Nova, and most academic clusters is SLURM (SLURM Workload Manager).

Figure 1.5 from Section 1.7 already has every piece. One idea is new: the scheduler is a queue in front of the compute nodes. You never pick a node yourself; you describe the job, and the scheduler decides where and when it runs.

ImportantKey idea

Real work runs as jobs: a script plus a resource request, started by the scheduler when a node is free, whether or not you are logged in.

Start with two machines. On the login node, ask what the machine is, then ask the same of a compute node from inside a small interactive session. Paste the srun line alone, and wait for the new prompt before typing anything else (Section 10.5 explains why). SCINet readers add -A <project> to srun, as in Section A.6:

Terminal (login node)
hostname; nproc; free -g | head -n 2
srun --cpus-per-task=2 --mem=2G --time=00:15:00 --pty bash -l
Terminal (compute node)
hostname; nproc; free -g | head -n 2
echo "cpus=$SLURM_CPUS_PER_TASK mem=$SLURM_MEM_PER_NODE"
scontrol show job "$SLURM_JOB_ID" | grep -E 'NumCPUs|mem=|QOS='
exit

10.2 Look at the cluster: nodes, partitions, accounts, QOS

Start hands-on. On the login node:

Terminal (login node)
sinfo -s
sacctmgr -n show assoc user=$USER format=account%30

On Ceres (captured 2026-11-21):

PARTITION AVAIL  TIMELIMIT   NODES(A/I/O/T) NODELIST
ceres*       up 60-00:00:0     110/8/39/157 ceres20-compute-[0-75],ceres20-mem-[0-10],ceres24-compute-[1-20],ceres25-compute-[1-50]
scavenger    up 21-00:00:0     110/8/39/157 ceres20-compute-[0-75],ceres20-mem-[0-10],ceres24-compute-[1-20],ceres25-compute-[1-50]
          <project>
          <project-2>

sinfo -s lists the partitions, groups of nodes with their own limits. The * marks the default, used when you do not name one. TIMELIMIT is the partition’s longest run (60-00:00:0 is 60 days, cut short by the column width). NODES(A/I/O/T) counts nodes that are allocated, idle, other (down or drained), and in total. sacctmgr lists your accounts, the projects or groups your jobs are charged to. Atlas lists atlas*, bigmem, four GPU partitions, development, and service, all infinite; Nova lists nova* (31 days), interactive (8 hours), instruction, scavenger, and a few others (Section A.6).

Then name it. A request names up to three things besides its resources:

  • a partition: which nodes may run it, and their limits;
  • an account: who is charged. On SCINet you must give one (-A <project>, or --account); Nova charges your default account if you leave it out;
  • a QOS (quality of service): another set of limits (longest run, most CPUs per user) attached to your account or to a partition.

Limits apply at several levels, and the strictest one wins (SLURM’s resource limits page). This is not academic. Ceres’s sinfo says 60 days, yet SCINet’s guide says jobs on ceres run at most three weeks. Both are right:

Terminal (login node)
scontrol show partition ceres | grep -o -E '(DefaultTime|MaxTime|DefMemPerCPU|QoS)=[^ ]+'
sacctmgr -n show qos format=Name%25,MaxWall,MaxTRESPU%40 | grep -E ' (ceres|normal) '
QoS=ceres
DefaultTime=02:00:00
MaxTime=60-00:00:00
DefMemPerCPU=2000
                   normal 21-00:00:00
                    ceres 21-00:00:00                 cpu=4000,mem=36T,node=32

The partition allows 60 days, but it attaches the ceres QOS to every job in it (QoS=ceres), and that QOS stops jobs at 21 days (MaxWall). The same two lines also show what you get when you leave things out: 2 hours (DefaultTime) and 2,000 MB per CPU (DefMemPerCPU). Table 10.1 has the three sites side by side; on two of the three, the number in sinfo is not your real limit.

Your own limits. Three commands answer “which limits apply to me?”. All three worked for an ordinary user on all three sites:

Terminal (login node)
sacctmgr -n -P show assoc user=$USER format=account,partition,qos,defaultqos
sacctmgr -n show qos format=Name%25,MaxWall,MaxTRESPU%40
scontrol show partition <name>

The first lists, per account, the QOS you may use and your default one (Atlas: debug,normal, default normal; Nova: normal,scavenger, default normal). The second gives each QOS’s limits. The third gives a partition’s own settings and the QOS it attaches, if any. Once you have a job, scontrol show job <jobid> shows the QOS it really got (QOS=ceres above; QOS=normal on Nova).

A request that breaks a limit is either refused when you submit or accepted and left waiting with a reason (Section 10.8), depending on how the site is set up. All three sites refuse it at once. Ceres, asking for 30 days (captured 2026-11-23):

Terminal (login node)
sbatch -A <project> --time=30-00:00:00 --wrap="hostname"
sbatch: error: QOSMaxWallDurationPerJobLimit
sbatch: error: Batch job submission failed: Job violates accounting/QOS policy (job submit limit, user's size and/or time limits)

No job was created. (--wrap turns one command into a tiny job without a script file, handy for tests.) The message names the QOS limit, which confirms where the 21 days come from. Atlas gave the same message for 30 days; Nova, whose limit is the partition’s, said Requested time limit is invalid (missing or exceeds some limit) for 40 days.

What one node has, in CPUs, memory (MB), and GPUs:

Terminal (login node)
sinfo -N -o "%N %c %m %G" | head -n 3
NODELIST CPUS MEMORY GRES
ceres20-compute-0 96 752000 (null)
ceres20-compute-0 96 752000 (null)

(A node in two partitions is listed twice.) A job cannot be bigger than one node unless it is written to run across several, which the book’s tools are not. Scavenger partitions run jobs on nodes nobody else is using, at no charge, but a job there can be stopped (preempted) when the owner’s work arrives; use them only for jobs that can be rerun.

Check yourself. sinfo shows 60 days for a partition, but the site’s guide says 3 weeks. How do you find which limit applies to your job?

TipAnswer

Look past the partition: scontrol show partition <name> shows whether it attaches a QOS, sacctmgr show assoc user=$USER … shows your account’s QOS, and sacctmgr show qos shows each QOS’s MaxWall. A real job’s QOS= field in scontrol show job tells you which one it got. The strictest limit wins; an over-long request is refused or left pending with a reason that names the limit.

10.3 Where files go: storage tiers and quotas

Start hands-on. Check your quota with the site’s command, then count the files in the book environment, in your cluster project:

Terminal (login node)
my_quotas                                  # SCINet
cat /work/<group>/group_storage_usage      # Nova: find your own row
cd /work/<group>/<netid>/bioinf-computing  # SCINet: /project/<project>/<user>/bioinf-computing
find .pixi -type f | wc -l
du -sh .pixi

On Ceres, my_quotas printed one line for your home, one per project, and one per project on Juno, the long-term archive, each with usage, quota, and status (captured 2026-11-20; Table A.5 has every site’s command). The environment, on Ceres and on Nova (captured 2026-11-23):

13001
1.3G    .pixi
13001
813M    .pixi

Thirteen thousand files, for one small environment. find -type f counts regular files only; the site’s own count (Nova’s OBJUSED column) also includes folders and links, which here bring the total to 15,528, so the two numbers need not match. Nova’s du says 813 MB for the same files because its /work compresses data.

Then name it. A cluster has several kinds of storage, each for a different job (Figure 10.1, Table 10.3):

  • Home (/home/<user>): small, 10–30 GB. Config files and the pixi program itself; never data, environments, or results.
  • Project or group (/project/<project> on SCINet, /work/<group> on Nova): large, shared with your lab, quota per project. Your project folder lives here, and so does the bioinf-shared/references folder from Section 9.7.1 that data/ref links to.
  • Scratch (/90daydata on SCINet, /ptmp on Nova): large and free, but purged: SCINet deletes files 90 days after last access; Nova’s banner says 60 days unused. Never backed up. For intermediate files you can make again.
  • Node-local temporary space: a disk inside the compute node itself, reached through $TMPDIR while your job runs. SCINet’s scratch guide and ISU’s file storage page say it is deleted when the job ends. It is often faster than network storage for many small files, but that depends on the site and the work.

Beyond these four sits the long-term archive: Juno on SCINet, /lss on Nova, which compute nodes on Nova cannot see.

What $TMPDIR is, per site. Ceres and Nova set TMPDIR=/tmp. In a batch job there, that /tmp is not the node’s shared one: it is an empty folder that belongs to you alone (Ceres, captured 2026-11-23):

TMPDIR: /tmp
drwx------. 2 <user> root 6 Nov 23 11:52 /tmp
/dev/nvme0n1p1  1.5T   11G  1.5T   1% /tmp

A shared /tmp reads drwxrwxrwt and belongs to root. SLURM on these two sites gives each job its own empty /tmp on the node’s 1.5 TB disk and removes it when the job ends. Atlas does it differently: a batch job gets TMPDIR=/local/scratch/<user>/<jobid>, a new empty folder per job on a 1.7 TB disk, while /tmp stays the node’s shared folder. Two jobs on the same Atlas node each saw only their own files. So scripts say $TMPDIR, never /tmp, and they work on all three. To see which kind your site has:

Terminal (login node)
scontrol show config | grep -i -E 'NamespaceType|JobContainer'

namespace/tmpfs (Ceres, Nova) means a private /tmp per job; (null) (Atlas) means no such plugin, so look at what $TMPDIR is set to.

Files, not only bytes. Many systems limit the number of files as well as the space, because each file costs the storage system work. A pipeline’s work folder can hold millions of small files (Chapter 26), and a few environments in home can fill a home quota of files long before its gigabytes. SCINet and Nova state no file-count limit (Section A.5), but Nova’s usage file reports OBJUSED, and many other sites do enforce one.

CautionMisconception: “If my quota shows free space, I can keep making files”

Reality. Many systems also limit the number of files. One environment is about 15,000 files; a pipeline’s work folder can be millions. Count with find … -type f | wc -l, and read the site’s own file count where it gives one.

10.3.1 Staging: work in your own temporary folder

Chapter 3 promised that large intermediate files could live in temporary storage while a job runs, with only the results kept in the project. Here is the pattern, complete, as a job script. Save it as jobs/ch10_staging.sbatch; SCINet readers replace <project> with their account, Nova readers delete that line. It sorts results/read_counts.tsv, which Chapter 8’s pipeline left in your project, by read count:

jobs/ch10_staging.sbatch
#!/usr/bin/env bash
#SBATCH --job-name=ch10_staging
#SBATCH --account=<project>       # SCINet; Nova: delete this line
#SBATCH --nodes=1
#SBATCH --ntasks=1
#SBATCH --cpus-per-task=1
#SBATCH --mem=1G
#SBATCH --time=00:05:00
#SBATCH --output=logs/%x_%j.out
set -euo pipefail
1project=$PWD
2out="$project/results/sorted_counts.tsv"
3tmp_root=${TMPDIR:?TMPDIR is not set}
4work=$(mktemp -d "$tmp_root/ch10_${SLURM_JOB_ID:-local}_XXXX")
echo "work folder: $work on $(hostname)"
cp "$project/results/read_counts.tsv" "$work/"
5LC_ALL=C sort -T "$work" -t $'\t' -k4,4n "$work/read_counts.tsv" > "$work/sorted.tsv"
6du -sh "$work"
7cp "$work/sorted.tsv" "$out.tmp" && mv "$out.tmp" "$out"
8rm -rf "$work"
ls "$work" || true
echo "done: $(wc -l < "$out") lines in $out"
1
A job starts in the folder you submitted from, so $PWD is the project when you submit from there (Section 10.6).
2
Output paths are absolute and decided before anything changes folder.
3
${TMPDIR:?message} gives the value of TMPDIR, or, if it is unset or empty, stops the script with that message. Its cousin ${VAR:-default} gives a default instead of stopping; both appear again below.
4
The job makes its own folder inside temporary space, with the job ID in its name; mktemp -d fills in XXXX so two jobs never collide.
5
sort -T tells sort where to write its own temporary files, the promise from Chapter 6: putting the input in a folder does not tell a tool where to keep its temporary files. -t $'\t' -k4,4n sorts on the fourth column, num_seqs, as numbers; the header stays on top because a word counts as 0 in a numeric sort.
6
Disk checks stay inside the job’s own folder.
7
The result goes back to project storage through a .tmp name, as in Section 8.10.
8
The job removes only the folder it made; never clean $TMPDIR as a whole, which other jobs on the node may share (Atlas).

Try it on the laptop first (macOS and Linux set their own TMPDIR, or the :? line tells you), from the project folder, then submit it on the cluster in the same way as the first job in Section 10.6:

Terminal (laptop)
cd ~/bioinf-computing
bash jobs/ch10_staging.sbatch

As a job on Nova (captured 2026-11-23):

work folder: /tmp/ch10_17734237_3FzT on nova18-71
8.0K    /tmp/ch10_17734237_3FzT
ls: cannot access '/tmp/ch10_17734237_3FzT': No such file or directory
done: 3 lines in /work/<group>/<netid>/bioinf-computing/results/sorted_counts.tsv

The ls error is the good news: the folder is gone. On the laptop the work folder was under macOS’s own temporary folder, and the result was the same three lines. Three more points about the pattern:

  • Commands that need the book environment run with pixi run from the project folder, or with pixi run --manifest-path "$project/pixi.toml" from anywhere else.
  • A job killed by its time or memory limit never reaches the copy-back line. Anything a long job must keep is written to project storage as it goes.
  • With a tiny file, this changes nothing about speed. It is the habit that matters, for the day the files are large and many.
WarningPitfall: thousands of small files move slowly

Moving 2 GB of R packages (67,000 small files) from home to /work on Nova with mv took hours and died when the SSH connection dropped (observed 2026-11-22). Network storage is slow per file. Run big moves as a job, or with rsync inside tmux (Section 9.5), which can resume.

The same rule from Chapter 3 still holds: cluster storage is not a backup. SCINet’s project storage is not backed up at all, and Nova’s snapshots are kept for days, not years.

Check yourself. Your project has 400 GB free, but an install fails with Disk quota exceeded. Give two possible causes.

TipAnswer

A file-count limit (the install makes many small files, and the site counts files as well as bytes); or the install is writing into your home, which has a much smaller quota (a cache or environment that defaults to ~). Check the site’s file count and where the files are going.

Terminal (login node)
module load samtools/1.23.1-py313-3cgtvqh   # SCINet: samtools/1.17
module list
type -a samtools
module purge
type -a samtools

After module load, type -a finds samtools in a folder from the module tree; after module purge, it is gone again (or only the book environment’s copy is found, if one is active).

Then name it. A module system (on these sites, Lmod) changes PATH and a few other variables, for this shell only (Section 7.4). Nothing is installed or removed. Three rules follow:

  • Load a named version. A bare module load samtools takes the site’s default, which changes when the site adds a version; Nova’s “Find First Rules” message above is Lmod saying the default was picked by position in the tree.
  • Load modules inside the job script, not in the shell you submit from: the script should say everything it needs. On Ceres and Nova the module command works in a batch job as it is (checked 2026-11-23); if your site’s batch jobs say module: command not found, its documentation gives a setup line for the top of the script.
  • Never in ~/.bashrc (Section 7.6): a module loaded at every login quietly changes every job and every pixi run.

The book’s way. Use pixi run for everything the book environment has, and a module only for what it lacks. Never both for the same tool in one job: whichever folder comes first on PATH wins, and type -a (Section 7.4) shows which. A module or a container (Chapter 24) can provide a tool the environment does not; how environments and modules compare in depth is Chapter 23.

TipLoad a version, not a default, inside the script

Write module load samtools/1.17, not module load samtools, as a line of the job script. Next year the default may be a different version; the named one either loads or fails loudly.

Check yourself. samtools --version prints different versions in your shell and in your job. Give two reasons.

TipAnswer

A module was loaded in one and not the other (in your shell from earlier, but not in the script, or a different version); or one ran pixi run samtools and the other the bare samtools, finding a different copy on PATH. type -a samtools in both places shows which copy each one found.

10.5 Interactive jobs

Start hands-on. Start the interactive session from Section 1.3 with your site’s options, pasted alone (SCINet: add -A <project>):

Terminal (login node)
srun --cpus-per-task=1 --mem=4G --time=00:30:00 --pty bash -l

When the compute node’s prompt appears:

Terminal (compute node)
hostname
echo "$SLURM_JOB_ID"

From a second terminal, logged in to the same cluster, list your jobs; then exit the session and list them again:

Terminal (login node)
squeue -u $USER

While the session is open, squeue shows one job, with the ID the session printed, a state R (running), the time it has run, and the node. After exit, the list is empty, on all three sites. The shell was a job all along: it had an ID, a time limit, and an allocation, and exit ended it.

Then name it. srun --pty bash -l starts a new job whose one command is a login shell, connected to your terminal (--pty). salloc works in two stages: it first reserves the resources, then you run commands in them. What happens after salloc differs by site (captured 2026-11-23). On Ceres and Nova you land on the compute node:

salloc: Granted job allocation 17706564
salloc: Nodes nova21-swift-4 are ready for job

and hostname prints nova21-swift-4. On Atlas, salloc holds the node but leaves you on the login node:

salloc: Granted job allocation 21323405
salloc: Nodes atlas-0006 are ready for job
atlas-login-1.hpc.msstate.edu
             JOBID PARTITION     NAME     USER ST       TIME  NODES NODELIST(REASON)
          21323405     atlas interact   <user>  R       0:05      1 atlas-0006
atlas-0006

Those three answers came from hostname, squeue -u $USER, and srun hostname: only the command started with srun ran on atlas-0006, as SCINet’s Ceres and Atlas differences page says. On every site exit released the allocation (salloc: Relinquishing job allocation …). Still, after any salloc, run squeue -u $USER and scancel <jobid> anything left over.

WarningPitfall: after salloc, check where you are

10.7 Asking for resources

Start hands-on. Read the request in jobs/01_count_reads.sbatch line by line against Figure 10.4:

jobs/01_count_reads.sbatch (the request)
#SBATCH --nodes=1
#SBATCH --ntasks=1
#SBATCH --cpus-per-task=1
#SBATCH --mem=2G
#SBATCH --time=00:10:00

One node, one task, one CPU for that task, 2 GB of memory, ten minutes.

Then name it. The scheduler reserves that box for your job, and enforces two of its sides:

  • Go over the memory, and the job is killed.
  • Go over the time, and the job is killed.
  • Ask for CPUs the tool does not use, and nothing breaks, but they sit idle, reserved for you and unavailable to anyone else.

CPUs do not set threads. How many threads a tool starts is decided by the tool’s own option, not by SLURM. seqkit uses 4 threads unless you tell it otherwise (its -j option, or the SEQKIT_THREADS variable; SeqKit usage). With --cpus-per-task=1, an unadjusted seqkit starts more threads than it was given, and they share one CPU; with --cpus-per-task=8 and no -j, it uses four and leaves four idle. Pass the request to the tool:

threads=${SLURM_CPUS_PER_TASK:-1}
seqkit stats -j "$threads" -T data/raw/*.fastq.gz

The array script in Section 10.10 does exactly this. (Threads and how much faster they make a tool are Chapter 35; the rule here is only “ask for as many CPUs as you tell the tool to use”.)

CautionMisconception: “If I ask SLURM for 8 CPUs, my tool uses 8”

Reality. SLURM reserves the CPUs; the tool starts as many threads as its own option says (seqkit: 4 by default, whatever you reserved). Pass $SLURM_CPUS_PER_TASK to the tool’s thread option.

The shape of a request. Most bioinformatics tools are one program using several threads, so the shape is always the same: --nodes=1 --ntasks=1 --cpus-per-task=N. --ntasks counts separate copies of a program (tasks), which programs written to run across many machines need and the book’s tools do not. What a “CPU” is depends on the site: a core, or one of a core’s two hardware threads (Figure 1.3). On Nova the first job asked for 1 CPU and got 2:

               JobID           State ExitCode    Elapsed     ReqMem     MaxRSS  AllocCPUS
            17720240       COMPLETED      0:0   00:00:02         2G                     2

Nova’s nodes have two hardware threads per core, and SLURM there hands out whole cores (scontrol show config lists SelectTypeParameters = CR_CORE_MEMORY), so an odd request is rounded up. Check AllocCPUS, not only what you asked for; seff’s CPU efficiency is measured against what you got.

Memory and time. Give memory with --mem (per node: for a one-node job, the whole job’s memory). --mem-per-cpu multiplies by the CPU count, which is easy to get wrong; use --mem. Times are D-HH:MM:SS or HH:MM:SS (--time=1-12:00:00 is a day and a half). Always set both. If you leave them out, you get the partition’s default, not “as much as needed”: 2 hours and 2,000 MB per CPU on Ceres, 2 hours and 7,686 MB per CPU on Atlas, 4 hours and 3,800 MB per CPU on Nova (scontrol show partition, Table 10.1). A job that needs 6 hours is killed at 2 on Ceres.

CPU efficiency, measured. The first job finished in two seconds, far too fast to show anything about CPUs (seff reported CPU Efficiency: 0.00%). Two longer test jobs on Ceres (captured 2026-11-23) each held 4 CPUs for two minutes: one kept all four busy, the other only one.

Both jobs held the same four CPUs for the same time; the second used a quarter of them. Section 10.9 works these numbers out by hand.

Why accurate requests matter. The scheduler does not simply run jobs in order. While it waits to start a big job, it fits smaller, shorter ones into the gaps, as long as they do not delay the big one (backfill; SLURM’s scheduling configuration). So a shorter, smaller request can start sooner. It is not guaranteed: priority, fair share, and new jobs arriving all change the picture. Try it in Figure 10.5: a toy node with 8 CPUs, three jobs running, and one big job waiting for the whole node. Set your job’s CPUs and requested hours, and see when it could start.

viewof bfCpus = Inputs.range([1, 8], {step: 1, value: 2, label: "Your job: CPUs"})
viewof bfHours = Inputs.range([1, 24], {step: 1, value: 2, label: "Your job: hours requested"})
bfStart = {
  // CPUs free on the toy node at hour t, with the big job's reservation from hour 3 to 13
  const free = (t) => t < 2 ? 2 : t < 3 ? 6 : t < 13 ? 0 : 8;
  const fits = (s) => {
    for (let t = s; t < s + bfHours; t += 0.5) if (free(t) < bfCpus) return false;
    return true;
  };
  for (const s of [0, 2, 3, 13]) if (fits(s)) return s;
  return 13;
}

If the figure does not load, the lesson is in Table 10.9: the same 2 CPUs start now when asked for 2 hours, and wait 13 hours when asked for 12.

And what it costs. Fair share, which lowers the priority of groups that used a lot recently, is based on what your jobs used while they ran (the resources they held × how long, weighted as the site chooses; SLURM’s multifactor priority page). Asking for 3 days and finishing in 10 minutes does not by itself charge 3 days; but CPUs you held and left idle while the job ran do count.

CautionMisconception: “Asking for lots of memory and time is the safe choice”

Reality. A big or long request can wait longer, because the scheduler can fit smaller, shorter jobs into gaps first; and resources you hold but do not use are unavailable to others and can count toward your group’s usage. Measure a test run and add a margin (Section 10.9).

Check yourself. A job asked for 16 CPUs and ran seqkit stats -j 2. How many CPUs did it use, and what should the request be?

At most 2; the other 14 sat idle. Request 2 (--cpus-per-task=2), or raise -j to match the request, written as -j "$SLURM_CPUS_PER_TASK" so the two cannot drift apart.

  • timeout: the job’s own line says TIMEOUT, but its batch line says only CANCELLED 0:15 (signal 15, the polite “stop” of Table 7.9). Here the reason is on the job’s line. The log’s last line, from SLURM, says DUE TO TIME LIMIT.

NoteBackground: reading ExitCode and COMPLETED

ExitCode is two numbers, status:signal. 127:0 is the script’s own exit status (here, “command not found”), with no signal. 0:15 and 0:125 mean the job was stopped from outside: by signal 15 when the time ran out, and by the out-of-memory kill. COMPLETED only means the script exited with status 0: it says nothing about whether the result is right. Check the result.

Read the records together. sacct gives one line for the job (the allocation line, which holds the request) and one per step: .batch is the script itself, .extern is SLURM’s own bookkeeping, and a script that runs commands with srun gets numbered steps too. Read those lines and the log, because the most useful line varies: for TIMEOUT it was the allocation line, for OUT_OF_MEMORY both, and for typo the log.

Then waiting. A pending job shows its reason in squeue’s last column, NODELIST(REASON). Above, the four jobs submitted together waited with (Priority); the first job of the chapter showed (None). Both are normal.

The two limit reasons appear only on sites set up to accept such jobs; all three of ours refuse them at submission instead (Section 10.2). For everything SLURM knows about a job, use scontrol show job <jobid>; squeue --start -u $USER gives an estimated start time, which is often empty.

CautionMisconception: “My job is pending, so something is wrong”

Reality. Priority and Resources are normal waiting; only some reasons (a limit the request breaks, a dependency that failed) need a fix. Read the reason column.

  • The step lines hold the use: MaxRSS 106944K on .batch, about 104 MB. MaxRSS is the peak memory of one step, sampled every so often, not a sum over everything in the job; a blank or zero means that line was not measured, or the site does not collect it.

CPU efficiency by hand. seff’s number is TotalCPU / (Elapsed × AllocCPUS): CPU time used, divided by CPU time held. For the two jobs of Table 10.8: 7 min 58.9 s is 478.9 s, and 478.9 / (126 × 4) = 95.0%; the second job’s 120.0 / (125 × 4) = 24.0%. Both match seff. For the two-second count above, the CPU time rounds to nothing, so 0% says only that the job was too short to measure.

Then estimate. Test on a small input, measure, and scale with care. A small run is not a fair sample (Section 1.5): memory often depends on the reference (an aligner’s index is the same size for one sample or a hundred), time on the number of reads. For the next run of a comparable job that succeeded, start from:

  • memory: the measured peak plus about 25%;
  • time: the measured time, scaled to the new input size, plus a margin.

That is a starting estimate, not a guarantee: sampling can miss a short peak, and a job killed for memory tells you only that it needed more than it was given, not how much. For disk, du -sh the outputs and the job’s temporary folder. On the GNU/Linux nodes of a cluster, /usr/bin/time -v <command> reports one command’s peak memory (“Maximum resident set size”) and time directly; macOS’s /usr/bin/time has no -v.

The habit. Record each job’s request, what it used, its job ID, and how long it waited in NOTEBOOK.md (Section 3.9). After a few runs of the same step, you will know its size without guessing.

Check yourself. A job on the same input as next time shows MaxRSS 1.2 GB against ReqMem 32 GB, and Elapsed 0:04:10 of a 2-day request. What do you request next time?

TipAnswer

About 1.5–2 GB of memory (the peak plus a margin) and well under an hour, say 20–30 minutes. The smaller request can start sooner and leaves the rest of the node for others.

10.11 Chains of jobs (overview)

Overview. Section 8.10 ran numbered scripts one after another with src/run_all.sh and promised a cluster version. The simplest one is a single job that runs run_all.sh: its order and its stop-at-the-first-failure behaviour stay exactly as they are. Make a copy of jobs/01_count_reads.sbatch that runs pixi run bash src/run_all.sh instead, and that is it.

Separate jobs earn their keep when steps need different resources (a big-memory step, a many-CPU step) or can run side by side. SLURM can start one job only after another succeeds:

Terminal (login node)
jid=$(sbatch --parsable jobs/01_count_reads.sbatch)
sbatch --dependency="afterok:$jid" jobs/02_gtf_summary.sbatch
squeue -u $USER

--parsable makes sbatch print only the job ID, which $( ) saves in jid. (jobs/02_gtf_summary.sbatch is a copy of the first job script that runs src/02_gtf_summary.sh; making it is exercise 5.) While the first job waits or runs, squeue shows the second as (Dependency). afterok imposes the order you chose; it passes no data. Here the two steps do not even need each other’s output, so afterok only says “not before”. If you did Chapter 8’s exercise 4, src/03_transcript_biotypes.sh can be submitted with no dependency at all, beside 02, and you can watch both run at once.

When the first job fails, afterok never starts the second. What happens to it then depends on the site (captured 2026-11-23, with a first job that failed):

  • Ceres and Nova leave it pending for ever, as (DependencyNeverSatisfied). Cancel it yourself with scancel, or submit it with --kill-on-invalid-dep=yes so SLURM cancels it.
  • Atlas cancels it automatically (its configuration has kill_invalid_depend): the second job went from PD (Dependency) to CANCELLED with no scancel.

So after a failed chain, check squeue: on some sites the job waits forever, on others it is gone. This is also where hand-made chains stop being fun. A workflow manager (Chapter 25) tracks which steps are done, reruns only what changed, and submits each step as a job for you; nf-core pipelines do exactly this on SLURM (Chapter 26).

Check yourself. The first job of a chain fails. What happens to the second?

TipAnswer

It never starts. Depending on the site it stays pending as DependencyNeverSatisfied (Ceres, Nova: scancel it) or is cancelled automatically (Atlas).

10.12 GPU partitions (overview)

Overview. A GPU is asked for like any other resource, with --gres (generic resources), and usually in a GPU partition:

jobs/gpu_test.sbatch (the request)
#SBATCH --partition=<gpu partition>
#SBATCH --gres=gpu:1

Inside the job, nvidia-smi shows the card you got. Only tools built for GPUs use one: deep-learning tools, NVIDIA’s Parabricks, some basecallers. An ordinary tool on a GPU node leaves the GPU idle while someone who needs it waits.

The exact form is site-specific (observed 2026-11-23):

  • Atlas has four GPU partitions, gpu-v100, gpu-a100, gpu-a100-mig7, and gpu-l40s, with QOS limits of 2 days (7 for a100). sinfo -o "%P %G" names the cards, for example gpu:nvidia_a100_1g.10gb:56 on the MIG partition (an A100 split into slices). Use the name sinfo shows, or plain gpu:1: the OnDemand form’s gpu:a100_1g.10gb:1 was refused with Requested node configuration is not available.
  • Nova has no separate GPU partition: GPU nodes sit in the default nova partition (and interactive, scavenger), and the card type goes in --gres, for example --gres=gpu:a100:1.
  • Ceres has no GPUs.

Check a request without submitting it with sbatch --test-only, which says when and where the job would start, or refuses it. GPUs in depth are Chapter 37.

10.14 Summary

  • A cluster is many computers behind a queue. The login node is for editing and submitting; work runs as jobs on compute nodes, started by SLURM when resources are free. Inside a job, the allocation (SLURM_*, scontrol show job), not free, says what you may use.
  • A request names a partition, an account, and sometimes a QOS; the strictest limit wins. sinfo is not always the real limit: check scontrol show partition and sacctmgr show qos.
  • Home is small; projects hold your work; scratch is big but purged; node-local $TMPDIR lasts one job. Count files as well as bytes. Stage work in a temporary folder of your own and copy results back.
  • Load modules by named version inside job scripts, never in ~/.bashrc; use pixi run for what the book environment has, and never both for one tool.
  • srun --pty bash -l and salloc are interactive jobs; check hostname after salloc, and give back what you hold. To SSH to a compute node: hold it, find it in squeue, connect with ProxyJump, scancel it.
  • A job script is a bash script with #SBATCH lines first. Submit from the project folder with mkdir -p logs done; the job starts there with a copy of your environment and no terminal. Check sacct, the log, and the result: COMPLETED only means exit 0.
  • Ask for CPUs × memory × time on one node, always setting --time and --mem; pass the CPU count to the tool; check AllocCPUS. Smaller, accurate requests can start sooner.
  • Failures read as OUT_OF_MEMORY 0:125, TIMEOUT (with .batch CANCELLED 0:15), or FAILED 127:0 for a missing command; read the allocation line, the step lines, and the log together. Priority and Resources are normal waiting.
  • Size the next run from the last: peak memory plus about 25%, time scaled to the input, recorded in NOTEBOOK.md.
  • A job array runs one script over many inputs; dry-run every task number, guard the row past the end, and give each task its own output. afterok chains jobs; a failed chain waits forever on some sites.
